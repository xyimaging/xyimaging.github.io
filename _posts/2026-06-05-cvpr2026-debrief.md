## The Problem With CVPR at This Scale

There are over 4,000 papers at CVPR 2026. Five parallel oral tracks run simultaneously. The poster hall takes 20 minutes to walk end-to-end at a normal pace. On Tuesday I went to three poster sessions, one oral session, and two workshop talks, and I still felt like I'd seen maybe 1% of what was happening.

CVPR has always been overwhelming, but this year the scale crossed a threshold where trying to track individual papers stopped being useful. What I actually wanted to know was different: what are the dominant patterns? Which research areas are accelerating? Where are the ideas genuinely new versus incremental? And — most practically — what does any of this mean for the research I'm actually doing?

So I built a structured analysis pipeline. I categorized all confirmed papers from the CVF virtual program, ran topic taxonomy analysis, pulled speaker notes from the slides I prepared, and tried to synthesize a picture that was more than just "here are some papers I liked."

This post is that synthesis. It's long. I've organized it so you can skip to whatever's most relevant to you, but if you're working in medical AI, VLMs, image registration, or reconstruction, I'd suggest reading those sections carefully — there's more going on there than a casual conference walk-through would reveal.

## CVPR 2026 by the Numbers

The headline: **over 4,000 accepted papers**, up from 2,878 in 2025. That's roughly a 42% increase in one year. The acceptance rate actually ticked down slightly to 25.4% (from 28.4%), which means submission volume grew even faster — 16,092 submissions.

**141 oral presentations** at a 3.4% oral rate. That's the competitive bar. For context: getting an oral at CVPR is meaningfully harder than getting an oral at many NeurIPS or ICLR cycles. The oral selection is where you see what the program committee genuinely thinks is frontier work.

The conference is also now the **second most-cited venue in all of science** according to Google Scholar Metrics — above Nature Medicine, above Physical Review Letters. That context matters when you're deciding where to submit.

## Oral Papers: What the Program Committee Thinks Is Frontier Work

141 orals, 3.4% rate. The 3D Vision cluster dominated with ~45 orals, followed by Segmentation/Detection (~30), and Generative (~20). Medical AI had ~15 orals at a 7.5% oral rate — roughly double the conference average. I'll come back to that.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/posts/cvpr2026-debrief/fig3_oral_breakdown.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" caption="Oral presentation distribution across topic clusters. Medical AI's 7.5% oral rate is more than double the 3.4% conference average, suggesting reviewers view medical AI contributions as unusually high-novelty relative to their volume." %}

**Best Paper: D4RT** (Google DeepMind / UCL / Oxford). This is a unified 4D reconstruction model — depth, 3D tracking, and camera pose estimation in a single feedforward transformer pass. Previous methods for this problem ran three separate models, each trained independently, combined in a pipeline. D4RT's single encoder + point-query decoder runs 18–300× faster than prior methods. On a TPU, you can estimate camera pose at ~200 FPS for 1 minute of video. The win here isn't just performance — it's the architectural statement that these tasks should be unified, not pipelined.

**Test of Time: ResNet and YOLO.** Well-deserved.

A few other orals worth knowing:

**SAM 3D** (Meta FAIR / Berkeley) extends SAM's promptable segmentation paradigm into 3D. Give it an image and a SAM-style prompt — a point, a box, a mask — and it generates a 3D mesh or point cloud of the specified object. It's what SAM did for 2D, now for geometry. Award candidate.

**Molmo2** (Allen Institute for AI) is the open-model story. It matches GPT-4V on video grounding and pointing tasks, releases all training data and model weights, and covers capabilities that proprietary models don't yet support well: video pointing, counting-by-pointing, multi-image reasoning. For anyone who needs a capable VLM they can actually run and modify, this is the landmark paper.

**FINER** (Tübingen / ETH) is a sobering evaluation paper. They construct fine-grained negative query pairs — questions where the object or attribute described is subtly not present — and show that every major VLM, including GPT-4V, drops 20–40 percentage points in accuracy on these queries compared to positive ones. This is a systematic failure mode that existing benchmarks don't test. If you're deploying a VLM in any safety-critical context, this paper is required reading.

**Fast-ThinkAct** compresses the chain-of-thought reasoning in vision-language-action models from ~250 text tokens down to 6 latent tokens, achieving a 9.3× inference speedup while maintaining performance on SimplerEnv. The "verbalizable" part is clever — the 6 latent tokens can be decoded back into text for interpretability. Real-time VLA deployment has been blocked by reasoning latency; this paper removes that blocker.

## Industry Labs: What Each One Is Actually Doing

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/posts/cvpr2026-debrief/fig4_industry_heatmap.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" caption="Industry lab coverage heatmap across research clusters, now including NVIDIA. Color intensity shows relative paper concentration. Google DeepMind is spread across all topics; Meta is concentrated in segmentation; Apple is strong on VLM evaluation and compression; Amazon's portfolio is organized around agentic visual AI; NVIDIA concentrates on Embodied AI/Robotics and the Physical AI platform." %}

### Google DeepMind

~38 main-track papers, Best Paper (D4RT), platinum sponsor. Google's strategy at CVPR 2026 is visible and coherent: **unify everything into feedforward models, build multimodal agents, push toward edge/XR deployment**.

D4RT is the clearest expression of the first goal. TIPSv2 (vision-language pretraining with enhanced patch-text alignment) and Back to Basics (revisiting noise prediction in diffusion training) represent principled architecture work. Ego2Web and GUIDE are web/GUI agent benchmarks grounded in egocentric video — the agentic push. MedGemma, presented at the Medical Computer Vision workshop, is a production medical VLM built on Gemma — significant in ways I'll discuss in the medical section.

**Back to Basics** deserves a standalone mention: the paper argues that x-prediction (predict the clean image directly) creates a training-inference mismatch that ε-prediction (predict the noise) doesn't, and that modern architectures do better with the original formulation. This is a rare case of a major lab saying "we got it wrong, here's why." If it holds up, it affects how the next generation of diffusion models is trained.

### Meta / FAIR

~22 papers, 2 award candidates. Meta's strategy is the **SAM family**: extend promptable segmentation to every modality and dimension. SAM 3D (3D geometry), SAM 3D Body (full-body human mesh recovery), and several video and instance segmentation extensions. Both SAM 3D and SAM 3D Body are award candidates.

The important signal is architectural: Meta is building a universal perception backbone that other tasks will plug into. SAM became foundational because it generalized across 2D contexts — Meta is betting the same will happen in 3D and with bodies. From an academic lab perspective, anything downstream of SAM (medical, 3D, video) will likely benefit from these extensions.

### Apple

14 papers, 2 award candidates. The pattern is distinctive once you see it: Apple is systematically defining evaluation standards and building toward unified on-device generation+understanding.

The two award candidates are STARFlow-V (video generation with normalizing flows) and a compression paper on learned image compression. AToken is the architectural contribution: a single tokenizer for both discrete (generation) and continuous (understanding) vision — a prerequisite for any-to-any multimodal models. UniGen-1.5 extends this to image editing via RL-based reward unification.

The benchmark papers (VSAS-Bench for real-time visual streaming, SO-Bench for structural output evaluation, AMusE for audio-visual multi-speaker evaluation) tell a story: Apple is positioning itself as the company that defines rigorous evaluation for modalities they care about. That's a smart long-term play.

### Amazon

~15 papers, organized the GRAIL-V workshop. Amazon's research is clearly products-first: **agentic visual AI aligned with AWS, e-commerce, and Alexa**.

CodeV (oral) is the flagship paper: it trains models to generate Python code + image tool calls for visual questions, then uses tool-aware policy optimization (RL) to improve which tools get invoked. It outperforms GPT-4V on geometry, counting, and spatial reasoning. The "code as intermediate representation" paradigm is interesting — it gives you interpretability (the code is readable) and compositionality (tools can be combined) for free.

ImageRAGTurbo is the retrieval paper: retrieval-augmented diffusion that does text-to-image in a single step, using retrieved reference images. For e-commerce product imagery, this is directly commercially applicable.

### NVIDIA

~25 papers, 3 orals. NVIDIA's CVPR presence encodes a specific claim about the future of AI: **Physical AI is the next frontier, and NVIDIA is building the entire stack to enable it.**

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/posts/cvpr2026-debrief/fig7_industry_bars.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" caption="Industry lab paper counts at CVPR 2026 (estimated). NVIDIA sits between Meta and Amazon in direct CVPR output but its strategic footprint is broader — its infrastructure underlies many other papers not credited to NVIDIA directly." %}

This isn't the same as what Google, Meta, or Apple are doing. Google builds foundational algorithms. Meta builds open perception backbones. Apple builds on-device inference. NVIDIA is building the *platform* — the simulation environment (Isaac Lab / Omniverse), the world models for physical environments (COSMOS), the robot foundation models (GR00T), and the research infrastructure that enables everything above to scale.

**GR00T N1.5**, NVIDIA's humanoid robot foundation model, appears across multiple papers. **NitroGen** (oral) is the most striking demonstration: it uses the GR00T N1.5 vision-action architecture to train a generalist gaming agent on 40K hours of YouTube and Twitch gameplay — with controller button overlays extracted automatically as action labels. 52% relative improvement on 1,000+ games. The generalization is the key signal: GR00T isn't just a robot policy, it's a vision-action foundation model that spans embodied AI broadly.

**PixelDiT** (oral, University of Rochester / NVIDIA) challenges the latent-space orthodoxy that everyone else takes for granted. Latent diffusion models (LDMs) compress images into a learned latent space before generating — PixelDiT shows that a properly designed pixel-space DiT matches latent methods at lower inference latency, with better fine-detail preservation on high-frequency textures. This is both a technical contribution and a signal that NVIDIA has research interest in the generation stack, not just the inference stack.

**3D PET/CT Report Generation** (NIH/NVIDIA collaboration) represents NVIDIA's medical AI footprint — modest in scale but significant as a signal that NVIDIA compute infrastructure is being integrated into clinical imaging pipelines.

What makes NVIDIA different from every other company in this analysis: they're not competing on model quality, they're competing on ecosystem completeness. The question isn't "is NVIDIA's model better than Google's?" It's "once Physical AI becomes the dominant paradigm, who owns the simulation-to-deployment pipeline?" CVPR 2026 is part of NVIDIA's answer to that question.

## VLM / LLM Trends

The VLM story at CVPR 2026 has several threads worth tracking.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/posts/cvpr2026-debrief/fig8_trend_subtopics.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" caption="Trend sub-topic breakdown. The VLM cluster is now the largest by highlight share; within it, the evaluation sub-trend and unified tokenization sub-trend are the dominant 2026-specific directions." %}

**The gap with proprietary models is closing fast.** Molmo2 (AI2) matching GPT-4V on video grounding with full open weights and data release is the clearest signal. The closed-model advantage on standard benchmarks is shrinking. This has an important downstream effect: if you're building medical AI or domain-specific AI on top of VLMs, open models are now a serious option.

**Evaluation is the frontier right now.** FINER, SO-Bench, WorldLens, AMusE, PAI-Bench — the most impactful papers at CVPR 2026 are evaluation papers that reveal systematic gaps in how we think models perform. FINER's 20–40pp accuracy drop under fine-grained negatives is alarming. If you suspect there's a systematic gap in how models in your domain are evaluated, building the benchmark that reveals it is a paper.

**Unified tokenization is arriving.** AToken (Apple) and Weaver both propose single tokenizers for both discrete (generation) and continuous (understanding) vision. This removes the separate encoder towers that current multimodal models require. The architectural implication is significant: any-to-any multimodal models — generate image from text, understand image, generate text from image, do it all in one model without modality-switching — become possible with a unified token space. This is probably 2–3 years away from being the default, but the pieces are appearing.

**Tool use is becoming the dominant paradigm for agentic vision.** CodeV (Amazon) using Python code as an intermediate representation for visual reasoning is the clearest example. The advantages are real: code is interpretable, composable, and provides a natural interface for calling specialized visual tools (object detectors, depth estimators, etc.) as needed. This is the same intuition behind program synthesis for math reasoning, applied to vision.

## Agentic AI at CVPR 2026

Agentic AI doesn't have a dedicated track at CVPR — it permeates everything. The common thread across all agent papers is **vision-action pretraining on internet-scale data**.

NitroGen (NVIDIA collaboration, oral) trains a generalist gaming agent on 40K hours of YouTube and Twitch gameplay. Controller overlays extracted from the video provide action labels. 52% relative improvement on 1,000+ games. The architecture is GR00T N1.5 — the same vision-action pretraining paradigm that Google uses for web agents and that robotics labs use for VLAs. Internet video is already an action dataset; the field is learning to exploit this.

Ego2Web and GUIDE (Google) use egocentric video of computer use to train web agents. Watch-and-Learn (Google) uses computer use recordings. VIRAL (humanoid loco-manipulation, oral) uses 64 GPUs of simulation to generate training data, then deploys zero-shot on real hardware for 54 continuous cycles. The sim-to-real story is increasingly about *scale of simulation*, not algorithm novelty.

The benchmark explosion is significant: PAI-Bench, AMusE, VS-Bench, VSAS-Bench — the evaluation infrastructure for agentic systems is growing rapidly. This usually precedes a consolidation of the field around standard tasks and metrics. We're about 1–2 years away from agentic AI having the same benchmark culture that VLMs developed around VQA and MMMU.

**Medical AI Agents** (~20 papers at CVPR 2026, estimated) are an emerging sub-cluster. Guideline2Graph converts clinical practice guidelines into executable agent workflows. Routing to Autonomous AI presents a framework for deciding when a clinical AI system should act autonomously versus escalate to a human. MedGRPO applies multi-task GRPO to surgical video understanding — the same DeepSeek-style reinforcement learning applied to clinical video. This is early but the direction is clear: clinical AI is moving from classification to autonomous action.

## Diffusion Models and Generative AI

The diffusion story in 2026 has three threads.

**Efficiency is the primary unsolved problem.** SURF achieves a 12.5× speedup over Wan 2.1 14B without retraining — by denoising at full resolution only for the first few steps (to retain the high-frequency "signature"), then doing bulk denoising at lower resolution, then a brief high-resolution detail pass. SeaCache and SenCache both achieve 2–4× inference speedups via spectral and sensitivity-based token caching. The field is rapidly closing the gap between research-grade video generation and real-time deployment.

**Principled revisiting is happening.** Back to Basics (Google) argues that the field made an architectural mistake in moving to x-prediction, and ε-prediction with modern architectures is strictly better. ChordEdit frames image editing as optimal transport and finds the minimum-energy "chord" geodesic in latent space — enabling 1-step edits that match 50-step methods. PixelDiT (NVIDIA / Rochester) challenges the latent-space orthodoxy: operates directly in pixel space via multi-scale tokens, eliminating the VAE bottleneck, competitive with SD3 at lower latency. These papers share the instinct that the accumulated empirical choices in diffusion model design should be re-examined carefully.

**Diffusion is spreading into agents and physics.** SPARK uses VLM knowledge to generate sim-ready 3D articulated objects. ProPhy applies progressive physical alignment to close the sim-to-real gap. VISTA (Google) is a test-time self-improving video generation agent. The boundary between generative and discriminative is dissolving: generation is increasingly being used as a reasoning tool, not just an output modality.

## Medical AI at CVPR 2026

This is the section I spent the most time on, and it's the one I think has the most actionable content for the lab.

The headline numbers: **~200 papers with medical relevance**, roughly 4.9% of all accepted papers. **15 oral presentations** — a 7.5% oral rate, 2.2× the conference average. **5 dedicated workshops**, including two new ones (Med-Reasoner and CV4Clinic). One full-day tutorial.

### What changed in Medical AI this year?

The short answer: Medical AI is following the general LLM trajectory with about an 18-month lag, and 2026 is the year that gap starts to close visibly.

In 2024, medical AI at CVPR was CLIP pre-training (BioViL-T, MedCLIP, CheXagent) — image-text alignment. In 2025, it was instruction-tuning (LLaVA-Med, PathChat, HuatuoGPT) — VQA and basic report generation. In 2026, it's **clinical intelligence**: models that don't just recognize findings but understand clinical context, follow guidelines, and generate actionable outputs.

The elevated oral rate (7.5% vs 3.4%) isn't an anomaly — it reflects a genuine shift in reviewer perception. Papers that combine clinical impact with technical novelty are getting oral spots that would have been harder to get three years ago. The new Med-Reasoner workshop (focused on VLM clinical reasoning with 12 papers and 5 invited talks) and CV4Clinic (clinical translation, human-AI collaboration) reflect the community's growing seriousness about deployment, not just accuracy numbers.

### Dedicated Oral Session: Medical Vision (Sunday 13:00–14:30)

CVPR 2026 had a dedicated 90-minute medical oral block — Oral Session 6C. Four papers back-to-back, and the thematic coherence was striking.

**DK-DDIL** (13:12, Xiamen U.) addresses the deployment reality that nobody wants to talk about: hospitals add new scanners, new protocols, new imaging vendors, and your model needs to adapt without forgetting what it learned before. They use adaptive dark-knowledge distillation — selective preservation of intermediate representations from prior domains, with a dynamic gating mechanism that decides what to keep. This is the continual learning paper that actually addresses clinical deployment constraints rather than benchmark settings.

**LATA** (13:37, EPFL) is the safety paper. It provides conformal uncertainty bounds for medical VLMs — statistically guaranteed coverage without retraining. The mechanism is Laplacian graph-based transductive adaptation at test time: the graph enforces local consistency among test samples, and conformal prediction wraps the output to give coverage guarantees. For any medical AI paper heading toward clinical deployment in 2026–2027, calibrated uncertainty is becoming an expected component, not an optional one.

**Medic-AD** (13:50, Samsung Research / CUHK) is the unified medical VLM. A single model that handles anomaly detection, VQA, clinical reasoning, and report generation across modalities. The "clinical intelligence" framing — beyond VQA accuracy to actual clinical decision support — is the right framing for 2026. Samsung builds ultrasound hardware. This paper is their signal that they're building VLM-powered ultrasound AI as a product direction.

**SegMoTE** (14:02) brings Mixture-of-Experts to medical segmentation at the token level. Rather than routing entire samples or tasks to different experts, each spatial token in the decoder independently selects its own expert. This handles scale heterogeneity — the liver is large and low-texture, small vessels are tiny and high-contrast — without explicit architecture engineering per organ. Extends SAM with only 17M new parameters. Trained on MedSeg-HQ (150K masks), it outperforms baselines trained on 10× more data.

### Medical Orals Outside Session 6C

**Learning Diffeomorphism for Medical Image Registration with Time-Embedded Architectures Using Semigroup Regularization** (Sunday 8:12, Matinkia & Ray) is the registration oral. The key contribution is semigroup regularization: a mathematical constraint that enforces, algebraically, that any composition of learned deformation fields satisfies semigroup properties. In practical terms: if you register frame A→B and frame B→C, the composed deformation A→C is guaranteed to be a valid diffeomorphism — no folding, no non-invertible regions. More on this in the registration section below.

**R²-Seg** (Saturday 13:25, Zhejiang U. / Cambridge) addresses training-free OOD tumor segmentation. Two-stage: a VLM uses anatomical reasoning to propose tumor candidates, then a statistical filter rejects implausible ones. No training on the target domain, no new annotations needed. "Training-free" is load-bearing here — it means you can deploy to a new hospital with a new tumor type without expensive re-labeling. Chunhua Shen's group, strong reception.

**MDCS-MoAME** (Saturday 8:25) is the cancer survival prediction oral. WSI is a gigapixel image — capturing long-range spatial dependencies is hard. They address this with multi-directional scanning combined with a Mixture-of-Experts block that routes between Attention (global context) and Mamba (linear-time long-sequence modeling). First Mamba-based MoE for pathology survival prediction; TCGA SOTA.

### Medical VLMs: How Medical AI Is Converging with the Broader Ecosystem

The most important structural shift in medical AI at CVPR 2026 is that **RL is entering medicine the same way it entered general LLMs**.

OraPO and SAT-RRG both apply preference optimization / GRPO to radiology report generation — to eliminate token-level factual errors. OraPO achieves MIMIC-CXR SOTA with only 10% of training data. 10× data efficiency for clinical AI is a genuinely important result — labeled radiology reports are expensive to produce. MedGRPO applies multi-task GRPO to surgical video understanding. The connection to DeepSeek-R1 style GRPO training is direct and probably intentional. Medical AI is catching up to general LLM training practices at an accelerating pace.

**Medic-AD** is the clearest example of clinical intelligence maturation. **MedGemma** (Google DeepMind, presented at the Medical CV workshop) is the industry signal — a production medical VLM built on Gemma, covering radiology, pathology, and ophthalmology. When Google keynotes a medical workshop with a production model, the field is transitioning from research to deployment.

**LATA** contributes something different: you can have the best-performing medical VLM in the world, but if its uncertainty estimates are uncalibrated — if it's confidently wrong — it's not deployable in clinical settings. LATA's conformal prediction wrapper solves this without retraining. This is becoming a baseline expectation, not an optional extra.

**Ultrasound-CLIP** (Zhejiang U. Hospital) is the paper most directly relevant to our lab. It's CLIP-style pre-training specifically designed for ultrasound: semantic-aware contrastive loss that accounts for speckle noise, acoustic shadows, and anatomy-specific semantics. The result is a foundation model that handles ultrasound's unique challenges without the domain mismatch from general medical CLIP models. Before training any ultrasound model from scratch, this should be evaluated as a backbone.

### Clinical Report Generation: 13 Papers, One Benchmark

Thirteen papers on radiology report generation at one conference. All of them benchmark on MIMIC-CXR. The meaningful differentiation happens along three axes:

**Longitudinal modeling**: DELRER, TIM, and BiOTPrompt all address the problem that radiology is temporal — patients have follow-up CXRs, and the report should describe *what changed*, not just what's there. DELRER uses disease trajectory tokens. TIM separates stable anatomy description from disease-change description and refines them iteratively. These are the papers that actually match how radiologists think.

**RL-based factual alignment**: OraPO and SAT-RRG both apply preference optimization to eliminate token-level factual errors. OraPO achieves MIMIC-CXR SOTA with only 10% of training data.

**New modalities**: 3D PET/CT Report Generation (NIH/NVIDIA) extends the paradigm to volumetric dual-modality input. F²-Assist (Hunan U.) generates fetal growth trajectory reports from serial ultrasound — directly useful for OB/GYN clinical workflows. These are the papers that suggest where this subfield is heading: every clinical imaging modality eventually gets its report generation system.

## Medical Image Registration

Registration is the area where I've been spending the most time, so this section is more detailed.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/posts/cvpr2026-debrief/med_fig6_registration_taxonomy.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" caption="CVPR 2026 medical image registration paper taxonomy. Five confirmed papers across three architectural paradigms: neural ODE / diffeomorphic, multimodal disentanglement, and RL-based policy optimization. The semigroup-regularized oral (Learning Diffeomorphism) is the headline contribution." %}

Five confirmed registration papers, one oral. The architectural trend is clear: **neural ODE / diffeomorphic formulations are replacing discrete-step VoxelMorph-style networks**.

**Learning Diffeomorphism with Semigroup Regularization** (Oral, Sunday 8:12) is the headline result. The problem with standard diffeomorphic registration networks is that while the single-step deformation is trained to be smooth and invertible, there's no guarantee that compositions of deformations — which arise whenever you chain multiple registrations — remain valid. The semigroup constraint fixes this algebraically: the set of deformations forms a semigroup under composition, meaning closure and associativity are enforced at training time. The result is a network that provably produces valid diffeomorphisms at any composition depth.

Why does this matter for 3D ultrasound stitching? Because stitching a 3D US volume from a sweep of 2D frames is exactly sequential deformation composition — you're registering frame 1→2, 2→3, 3→4, and composing them into a single coordinate mapping. If any intermediate deformation folds or becomes non-invertible, the final 3D volume has topological errors. The semigroup guarantee eliminates this failure mode. This is the registration technique I'm most interested in adapting.

**Disentangle-then-Align** (Poster, Saturday 10:45) addresses multimodal registration — the case where you're aligning images from different modalities (MRI-CT, T1-T2, or multi-contrast ultrasound). It disentangles modality-specific style from anatomy-consistent content at multiple scales, then uses the anatomy features for non-iterative registration. Non-iterative is the key qualifier: standard multimodal registration methods run 10–50 optimization iterations per image pair at test time. Non-iterative means a single forward pass — necessary for real-time intraoperative imaging.

**MorphSeek** (Poster, Saturday 15:45) frames deformable registration as a sequential decision problem and uses policy gradient in latent space to optimize deformation fields. RL-based registration isn't new, but operating at the latent representation level (rather than pixel level) avoids the curse of dimensionality. The convergence speed advantage over pixel-space RL is substantial.

**Volumetrically Consistent Implicit Atlas Learning via Neural Diffeomorphic Flow for Placenta MRI** (Poster, Saturday 6:30, MIT CSAIL / Boston Children's Hospital) is the atlas construction paper. Implicit neural representation + diffeomorphic flow + volumetric consistency constraint to build anatomy-specific atlases from small clinical datasets. The placenta is a challenging target — it changes shape dramatically across gestational age and has high inter-subject variability. The method works with ~50 training cases. This framework is directly applicable to building organ-specific atlases for any anatomy where you have limited data.

The registration landscape at CVPR 2026 confirms a direction I've been watching: the field is moving from pixel/voxel-space optimization (classical) to architecture-level guarantees (semigroup regularization, neural ODEs). The guarantees are what enable clinical deployment — a neurologist needs to trust that the registered brain MRI doesn't have folded topology.

## Medical Inverse Problems and Reconstruction

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets/img/posts/cvpr2026-debrief/med_fig7_inverse_taxonomy.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1" caption="Medical inverse problems at CVPR 2026, plotted by modality vs. core method type. Discrete tokenization/VQ and physics-informed methods are the two dominant approaches. Gaussian splatting entering medical imaging is new for 2026." %}

Nine confirmed papers. The modality spread is heavy on MRI (six papers) with CT reconstruction and vessel reconstruction making up the rest.

**PTF-CT** (Saturday 6:30, CUHK) is the sparse-view CT reconstruction paper most directly relevant to 3D X-ray work. The key insight is doing iterative reconstruction in polar coordinates: fan-beam and cone-beam CT projections are naturally polar, so processing in that domain avoids the Cartesian-grid aliasing artifacts that affect FBP and most DL-based methods. The temporal-frequential decomposition handles the aliasing at multiple frequency scales. The result outperforms both FBP and non-iterative DL baselines at high undersampling rates. For a lab working on 3D X-ray/CT reconstruction, the polar coordinate processing idea is worth implementing.

**Prospective Dynamic 3D MRI Reconstruction via Latent-Space Motion Tracking from Single Measurement** (Friday 9:45, Michigan/Stanford) is the result that stopped me in the poster hall. Single k-space measurement per time frame → full 3D volume. 100× acceleration over standard acquisition. The mechanism: implicit neural representation for the 3D volume, plus a continuous latent motion trajectory that models inter-frame deformation. The INR provides a continuous 3D function you can query at any voxel position; the motion trajectory warps that function to match each observed measurement. This is prospective — it accounts for motion planning during acquisition, not just retrospective correction. The framework generalizes: anything with a 3D structure that moves (cardiac, respiratory, fetal) can benefit from this. Ultrasound 3D reconstruction under probe motion is the obvious extension.

**Adaptive Anisotropic Gaussian Splatting for Multi-contrast MRI Super-Resolution** (Friday 9:45) is the first application of Gaussian Splatting to medical 3D super-resolution at a major venue. Anisotropic Gaussians fit to anatomy — elongated along fiber bundles in white matter, spherical in fluid regions — guided by anatomy labels. Arbitrary-scale output and multi-contrast synergy are the practical advantages. 2026 is the year Gaussian Splatting moves from NVS into medical reconstruction.

**Solving a Nonlinear Blind Inverse Problem for Tagged MRI with Physics and Deep Generative Priors** (Sunday 14:30, Johns Hopkins) demonstrates the physics + score-based diffusion combination for a genuinely hard inverse problem. Tagged MRI is used for cardiac motion estimation — the forward model is nonlinear, the problem is blind (the tag geometry is unknown), and the data is severely undersampled. Combining explicit physics with a score-based diffusion prior as a plug-and-play regularizer produces estimates that classical methods can't match.

The two discrete tokenization papers — **Breaking the Continuum** and **Virtual Full-stack Scanning of Brain MRI** (Monash/Imperial) — represent a paradigm shift worth watching. Both replace continuous regression-based reconstruction with VQ-VAE codebook + masked token prediction. The advantage is perceptual quality: continuous MSE produces blurry reconstructions; discrete distribution learning can produce sharp textures. The Brain MRI imputation paper handles arbitrary missing contrasts from any available subset — one model for all 10+ MRI contrasts, any missing pattern.

## What This Means for the Research I'm Doing

A few concrete things I'm walking away from this conference with.

**Foundation models for ultrasound exist now.** Ultrasound-CLIP is there. Before running any ultrasound segmentation, classification, or retrieval experiment, evaluate Ultrasound-CLIP as a baseline. This is the kind of thing that saves months of pre-training work.

**Semigroup-regularized diffeomorphic registration is the registration method I want to use for 3D stitching.** The guarantee of valid deformation composition at any depth is exactly what's needed for sequential US frame registration. The implementation should be straightforward — it's an additional regularization term on top of an existing diffeomorphic network. The first thing to try.

**RL is entering medical AI for real.** OraPO, SAT-RRG, MedGRPO all apply GRPO or similar preference optimization. The mechanism is the same as DeepSeek-R1 — LLM-style RL training applied to medical tasks. If you have a task with a clear evaluable objective (clinical accuracy, factual correctness), RL-based training is now a legitimate technique to consider.

**Evaluation gaps are papers.** FINER revealed a 20–40pp accuracy drop in all VLMs under fine-grained negatives. WorldLens showed all driving world models fail on ≥2 evaluation axes. If you know of a systematic gap in how models in your domain are being evaluated, building the evaluation paper is a viable contribution — and it's getting into CVPR highlights.

**Medical AI is not crowded relative to its impact.** The 7.5% oral rate is evidence. If research can be framed medically — and most registration, reconstruction, and segmentation work can — the reception at CVPR is disproportionately warm relative to the difficulty of the contribution. This is likely to change in 2–3 years as the area grows, but the window is open now.

**The unification trend is a design challenge.** D4RT winning Best Paper for collapsing a three-model pipeline into one is a signal about what the program committee values. Looking at the current work: is there a multi-step pipeline that could be unified? The cost of unification is often high (training, data), but the payoff in terms of architectural elegance and publication impact is real.

**NVIDIA's Physical AI strategy has direct implications.** GR00T N1.5 is a vision-action foundation model, not just a robot policy. If you're building any system that involves perception + action in a physical environment, the GR00T pretraining paradigm is the infrastructure layer to understand. This affects how I'm thinking about the ultrasound guidance project — the perception-action loop from probe positioning to image acquisition is, at some level, an embodied AI problem.

One thing that struck me at this conference: the knowledge graph above shows Medical AI as relatively isolated from Embodied AI. There's no strong cluster of medical robotics papers connecting these two areas. That's surprising given that surgical robotics is an obvious intersection, and it suggests an underexplored opportunity. The tools (VLMs, world models, sim-to-real) exist — the applications to surgical robotics just haven't materialized at CVPR yet.

## Final Thoughts

CVPR 2026 confirms a few things that were already suspected and reveals a few that weren't.

**Confirmed:** VLMs are the new default architecture. Unified feedforward models beat pipelines. Scale of data and simulation now matters as much as algorithmic novelty. Open models are catching proprietary ones.

**Surprising:** Medical AI's oral rate is that high. The registration community has produced a paper with a mathematical guarantee on deformation validity. Discrete tokenization is appearing in medical reconstruction. Google is keynoting medical workshops with production models. NVIDIA is showing up at CVPR not to present a model but to demonstrate a platform.

**Uncertain:** Whether the principled revisiting of diffusion fundamentals (Back to Basics, ChordEdit) will reshape training recipes at scale. Whether the benchmark explosion in agentic AI will successfully characterize capabilities or just fragment evaluation further. Whether medical robotics will appear at CVPR in meaningful volume in 2027. Whether NVIDIA's Physical AI platform bet will make simulation-based training the default — or whether the real-world data approaches from Google and Meta will win.

The conference left me with more to read than I expected to find, which is probably the right outcome.
